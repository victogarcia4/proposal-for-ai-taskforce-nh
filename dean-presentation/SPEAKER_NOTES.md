## Slide 1 - Invite the campus into the conversation

Good [morning or afternoon], and thank you for making time for this conversation.

Today I am introducing the North Harris AI Use Norms consultation application. Its purpose is straightforward. It gives our campus a structured, respectful way to listen before we make or revise decisions about artificial intelligence.

AI is already affecting how people teach, learn, assess, advise students, manage information, and complete daily work. Because the impact is broad, the conversation needs to be broad as well. This application is designed to make that possible without asking everyone to attend the same meeting or complete a one-size-fits-all survey.

The central idea is simple: we invite people to share their experience, we organize those perspectives around focused questions, and human leaders use that information to guide the next steps. The application does not write policy or make decisions on behalf of the college. It supports a human-led consultation.

My request today is that we consider how deans can help distribute this invitation so that the people closest to teaching, student support, and operational work are represented in the conversation.

## Slide 2 - The leadership case

The leadership case for this application begins with a practical reality. AI use is already part of daily work across higher education.

In classrooms, faculty are deciding how students may use AI, how to assess learning, and how to maintain meaningful academic work. In student services and administrative areas, staff are considering where AI may be helpful, where human judgment must remain central, and how information should be protected. In every area, people are trying to make thoughtful decisions while the technology and its uses continue to change.

That is why one conversation must include many employee roles. Faculty, staff, facilitators, and leaders each see different opportunities, responsibilities, and risks. Students do not participate in this consultation. A single survey response or a single committee meeting cannot capture all of that context.

The application gives us a visible record of participation. It preserves contributions, allows people to return and revise their thinking, and supports a clear response back to the campus. This is important because people are more likely to participate when they can see that their contribution enters a real process rather than disappearing into an inbox.

Instead of treating consultation as one event, we can treat it as an ongoing, organized way to listen and learn together.

## Slide 3 - Institutional resources and the evidence gap

Before we discuss the participant experience, I want to explain why this consultation is justified alongside the resources Lone Star already provides.

The OTS AI Guidelines explain institutional expectations for responsible exploration. They address sensitive information, the review of generated content, and academic expectations. The AI Tools page provides a comparison tied to tool authorization and data levels. In the version reviewed on October seventh, the page describes Copilot for Microsoft 365 as in testing and lists ChatGPT and Claude as unapproved for internal use.

These are useful resources. Their purpose is to guide decisions about tools and appropriate use. However, neither page presents findings about the current experience of the North Harris community. The existence of guidance cannot tell us how familiar people are with it, how they interpret it, or which situations they find difficult in their work.

The tools page also contains status language that can change over time. I did not find an update date in the page text reviewed. That means we should ask OTS to confirm current authorization and availability. It would be premature to declare all of the guidance obsolete, and a newer or more capable model does not automatically become an approved institutional tool.

The evidence gap is specific. We have published expectations, but these two pages do not tell us what North Harris participants currently know, believe, or use. Our consultation can help investigate that gap while respecting the institutional guidance.

## Slide 4 - Why investigate North Harris?

There are three distinct questions that justify a local inquiry.

The first concerns opinions. What benefits do participants see? What worries them? Which proposed limits seem appropriate, and why? The reasons behind those positions can help leaders distinguish a training need from a disagreement about academic or workplace practice.

The second concerns knowledge. What do participants understand about approved tools, protection of information, and the need to check AI output? We should distinguish confidence from demonstrated knowledge. A person can feel comfortable using a tool while remaining uncertain about institutional expectations. The app can organize reported uncertainty and requests for clarification. A formal test of knowledge would require a separate instrument and validation.

The third concerns current use. Which tools and tasks do participants report using? What helps them, and where do they encounter problems? These are questions for the inquiry. We should verify that the collection prompts capture these details before claiming that the app measures tool adoption or frequency comprehensively. We should also avoid asking people to include student records or other sensitive information in examples.

This information can help North Harris choose training topics, clarify confusing expectations, and develop draft norms grounded in the work people actually describe. The app contributes an organized consultation process, with six working tables, saved contributions, and human review. It also allows people to explain their reasoning and revise their input as the conversation develops.

There is an important limit. Participation is voluntary. Responses describe those who choose to participate. They cannot establish how common a practice is across the whole campus. Administrators can link accounts to participation, so some people may hesitate to describe sensitive experiences. Other members receive generic author labels. We should report these limits, invite participation across roles and areas, and avoid treating the consultation as a compliance audit.

If the project develops into formal research, we will need an appropriate study design and institutional review for that purpose. For the consultation, the immediate goal is to learn from participants and return with useful, accountable next steps.

That is the justification for inviting our community: local evidence can make institutional guidance easier to apply, and it can help leaders decide what support North Harris needs next.

Source basis: [OTS AI Guidelines](https://www.lonestar.edu/OTS-AI-Guidelines) and [OTS AI Tools](https://www.lonestar.edu/OTS-AI-Tools). These questions and methodological limits form a proposed inquiry, not evidence that a campus-wide knowledge or adoption study has already occurred.

## Slide 5 - Purpose

This slide summarizes what the application does.

First, a participant enters through verified institutional access. That means the consultation is open to people with a Lone Star institutional email address, rather than to anonymous or unrelated accounts.

Second, the participant sees guided prompts organized in six working tables. The tables give people an easier way to find the question that is closest to their experience. Someone working in assessment does not have to begin with a question about data privacy. Someone working in student services does not have to begin with a question about classroom assignments.

Third, participants have control over their own input. They can save work, return to it, revise it, and see their saved contributions. This matters because thoughtful input does not always happen in one sitting.

Finally, the application supports human synthesis. Committee members and administrators remain responsible for reviewing themes, discussing positions, identifying evidence needs, and drafting next steps.

So the application is not an automated policy generator. It is a bridge between campus participation and accountable human review.

## Slide 6 - Designed for trust

For a consultation to be useful, participants need to understand who may take part, what information is required, and who remains accountable for decisions.

The application begins with verified institutional access. Participants use a Lone Star email address to receive a sign-in code. That creates a practical eligibility check without requiring another password.

The next requirement is a profile. A participant provides a name before contributing. The profile can also include work-related context, such as an employment category or discipline cluster, but those attributes do not give a person extra authority. Roles and permissions remain under administrative control.

Most importantly, people retain the decision-making role. Administrators manage participation. Committee members conduct the coding and synthesis. Academic leaders decide how to respond to the themes that emerge. The application supports that work. It does not replace it.

This is the trust principle behind the design: participants should be able to contribute in context, and the institution should remain accountable for how contributions are considered and what happens next.

## Slide 7 - A simple start

This screen shows the first action a participant takes.

The participant enters an institutional email address. The application checks that the address uses an eligible Lone Star domain. The participant then receives a sign-in code by email and enters that code in the application. No separate password is required.

After verification, the participant completes a profile by entering a name. The purpose of the profile is not to create a complicated enrollment process. It is to make sure the consultation includes identifiable, eligible campus participants and that their own saved work can be returned to them.

From a participant's perspective, the starting experience is designed to be clear. Enter an institutional email. Use the sign-in code. Complete the profile. Then begin participating.

The image on this slide uses a fictional preview email address. The application also includes a public demonstration workspace with fictitious data, which makes it possible to show the workflow without exposing real participant information.

## Slide 8 - Working tables

After the profile is complete, the participant chooses a working table.

The six tables reflect major areas where AI use is already raising practical questions. They include teaching, learning, and assessment; academic integrity and intellectual property; data privacy, security, and tools; workplace use and human accountability; equity, accessibility, and professional development; and governance, implementation, and evaluation.

The value of this structure is that a participant does not need to answer every question in the consultation. They can begin with the question closest to their experience.

For example, a faculty member might begin with learning outcomes or AI expectations in coursework. A staff member working with student records might begin with privacy and tools. A dean or administrator might focus on governance, implementation, or evaluation.

The working tables also help the college organize input without flattening it. Contributions remain connected to the topic that gave them meaning. That makes later human review more manageable and more transparent.

## Slide 9 - Table 1 questions: Teaching, Learning, and Assessment

The first working table concerns teaching, learning, and assessment. Its intended outcome is to help North Harris identify thoughtful criteria for AI use in coursework and examples of assignment design.

The application presents four questions. The wording on the slide matches the current question bank.

Question T1Q1 asks: “Which learning outcomes must students demonstrate through their own thinking and performance, and where does AI accelerate, replace, or distort the skill being taught?”

Question T1Q2 asks: “Which AI uses should be prohibited, permitted, or required at each stage of an assignment?”

Question T1Q3 asks: “What evidence should demonstrate a student's learning process when AI is used?”

Question T1Q4 asks: “What discipline-specific activity would develop responsible AI skills for students' future work?”

Participants can select the question closest to their teaching context. They are not expected to answer all four.

## Slide 10 - Table 2 questions: Academic Integrity, Transparency, and Intellectual Property

The second working table addresses academic integrity, transparency, and intellectual property. It asks how to make expectations understandable and how to review concerns fairly.

Question T2Q1 asks: “What should students and employees disclose about their use of AI?”

Question T2Q2 asks: “What minimum elements should every North Harris syllabus AI statement contain, and what rule should apply when a syllabus or assignment is silent?”

Question T2Q3 asks: “What evidence and review steps should be required before a finding of AI-related academic misconduct, given that detection tools alone are not reliable evidence?”

Question T2Q4 asks: “What safeguards should apply when AI is used with copyrighted materials, research, or institutional publications?”

These questions invite shared expectations while keeping existing institutional procedures in view.

---

## Slide 11 - Table 3 questions: Data Privacy, Security, and Tools

The third working table focuses on data privacy, security, and tools. It is intended to identify data and use considerations, questions for OTS, and a clear tool request process.

Question T3Q1 asks: “Which institutional data may be entered into each category of AI tool?”

Question T3Q2 asks: “What information must a vendor provide before an AI tool is considered for institutional use?”

Question T3Q3 asks: “What process should employees follow to request, pilot, and review an AI tool, including AI features that appear inside software LSC already uses?”

Question T3Q4 asks: “What should happen when an AI tool exposes data or materially changes its features or terms?”

Examples should stay general. Participants should not enter confidential or identifiable information into their responses.

---

## Slide 12 - Table 4 questions: Workplace Use, Student Services, and Human Accountability

The fourth working table asks where AI might support workplace or student-service tasks and where a person must remain accountable.

Question T4Q1 asks: “Which workplace or student-service tasks would benefit from an AI pilot?”

Question T4Q2 asks: “Which decisions must remain under a qualified employee's authority?”

Question T4Q3 asks: “How should people be informed when they interact with AI or receive an AI-assisted decision?”

Question T4Q4 asks: “What conditions, including workload, training time, and job security, would make an AI pilot acceptable to the employees whose work it affects?”

The table makes room for both potential benefits and the working conditions needed for a responsible pilot.

---

## Slide 13 - Table 5 questions: Equity, Accessibility, and Professional Development

The fifth working table focuses on access and professional development. It explicitly includes faculty, adjuncts, and staff.

Question T5Q1 asks: “What barriers prevent faculty, adjuncts, or staff from taking part in responsible AI use?”

Question T5Q2 asks: “What equivalent alternatives should be available when an AI tool is inaccessible, unaffordable, or declined?”

Question T5Q3 asks: “What AI competencies should employees demonstrate in their particular roles?”

Question T5Q4 asks: “What training formats, time, and recognition would make participation practical across schedules and employment categories?”

Answers can help identify support that works across roles and schedules.

---

## Slide 14 - Table 6 questions: Governance, Implementation, and Evaluation

The sixth working table addresses governance, implementation, and evaluation. It is intended to clarify who decides, where questions should go, and how the college can review progress.

Question T6Q1 asks: “Which norms should apply across North Harris, and which decisions should remain with a discipline, a unit, or an individual instructor?”

Question T6Q2 asks: “Which norms can North Harris adopt itself, and which must be referred to OTS or the system policy process?”

Question T6Q3 asks: “What indicators and evidence should show whether a norm or pilot is working?”

Question T6Q4 asks: “How often should the norms be reviewed, and what process should handle exceptions, unresolved disagreements, and requests for change?”

The committee can recommend and organize findings. Decisions that belong to OTS or the system policy process must go through those channels.

The application contains twenty-four questions across six tables. No participant is expected to address all twenty-four.

## Slide 15 - Simulated app walkthrough

This slide provides a short simulation of the participant journey. The screens shown here come from the application's public demo, which uses fictional information only.

The first screen is the profile. After an institutional email is verified, the participant enters a name and any requested contextual information.

The second screen is the working-table selection. The participant selects the table and question that best match their work or concern.

The third screen is the proposals and discussion area. Here, a participant can support an idea, support it with changes, or offer a reasoned concern or objection. The goal is not to force agreement. The goal is to make the reasons behind different positions visible.

The fourth screen is the participant's saved contributions. A person can return to their work, revise or expand a contribution, and see relevant committee responses as the consultation continues.

The key message for potential participants is this: they do not have to complete everything in one visit, and they do not have to agree with every proposal in order to participate constructively.

## Slide 16 - Close the loop

Asking for participation is only the first responsibility. The next responsibility is to show people what happened with their input.

The application is designed around a visible feedback loop. A participant begins with a contribution. Human reviewers then code and discuss themes. Those themes can inform draft norms and a review process. The college can then return with a clear summary of what was heard and what actions will follow.

This matters because consultation loses credibility when people cannot see a response. The application gives participants a place to revisit their saved input, while it gives the institution a structure for returning with synthesis, drafts, and follow-up actions.

The result is not necessarily immediate agreement. The result is a more accountable process. People can see that the campus heard multiple perspectives, considered them through human review, and identified the next step.

That is the practical difference between collecting comments and building a sustained campus conversation.

## Slide 17 - A simple dean action

I would like to close with a practical request for academic leaders.

First, please share the consultation link with faculty, staff, and student-facing teams in your areas. A broad invitation is essential if we want the consultation to reflect the real diversity of work across North Harris.

Second, identify a liaison within each area. The liaison does not need to become a technical expert. Their role is simply to help colleagues understand why the consultation matters, where to begin, and where to direct questions.

Third, set a visible participation window. People are more likely to contribute when they know there is time to reflect and return, rather than feeling that they must respond immediately.

Finally, help us return with findings. The commitment is not only to ask people for input. The commitment is to explain what was heard, what requires further discussion, and what actions or draft norms will come next.

With your support, this application can help North Harris begin a campus conversation that is more representative, more transparent, and more useful for responsible decision-making about AI.

Thank you.
